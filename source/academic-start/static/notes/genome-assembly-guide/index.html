🧬 Start Here: Building a Genome From Nothing

You know graphs, probability, and optimization. It turns out genome assembly is a graph and optimization problem wearing a biology costume. This guide translates the whole modern genome-building pipeline into language that leans on your existing math instincts rather than assuming biology background — and now also untangles a confusion almost everyone has: the difference between "a genome" and "the reference genome."

The one-sentence version

You shred a genome into millions of short, overlapping fragments, use algorithms to reconstruct the original sequence from the overlaps, quality-check the result three independent ways, and then — separately — decide how that one reconstructed sequence gets used as a shared "ruler" (the reference) against which every future individual of that species gets compared.

1 · Why This Is Hard: A Combinatorics Problem With Biology Attached

Strip away the biology and genome assembly is: given a large multiset of short, possibly-erroneous, overlapping substrings of an unknown string S, reconstruct S. This is closely related to the classic computer science Shortest Superstring Problem — which is NP-hard in general. Real assemblers don't solve it exactly; they use graph heuristics, discussed in Section 4.

Scale, for context

Human genome

~3.1 billion base pairs (letters), split across 23 chromosome pairs.

Galápagos tortoise

Roughly comparable scale to other reptile genomes — on the order of ~2 billion base pairs.

A single sequencing read

Ranges from ~150 letters (older short-read tech) to tens or hundreds of thousands of letters (modern long-read tech) — see Section 2.

So even a "long" read of 20,000 letters only covers about 0.001% of a 2-billion-letter genome. You need millions of reads, covering every position multiple times over, and then a way to stitch them back together correctly.

The three enemies of correct assembly

Enemy 1 — Repeats

If a 5,000-letter repeated sequence appears in 40 different locations in the genome, and your reads are only 2,000 letters long, then no single read can span the whole repeat — every read that lands inside it looks identical regardless of which of the 40 copies it actually came from. The assembler literally cannot tell them apart from sequence alone. This is why read length matters so much: a read longer than the repeat can "see past it" on both sides and anchor the repeat's true location.

Enemy 2 — Diploidy (two similar-but-different copies)

At most positions, your maternal and paternal chromosome copies (haplotypes) are identical. But at some positions they differ slightly (heterozygous sites). A naive assembler can get confused and either (a) randomly mix reads from both copies into one artificial "mixed" sequence, or (b) accidentally build two separate, redundant contigs for what's actually one region with two haplotypes, artificially inflating the apparent genome size. Modern haplotype-aware assemblers try to explicitly keep the two copies separate — this is called phasing. (We come back to this in full depth in Section 21.)

Enemy 3 — Sequencing error

Every sequencing technology has some error rate. If reads are long but error-prone, a stretch that should read identically across 10 overlapping reads might show 10 slightly different versions, purely from machine noise — and the assembler has to statistically figure out the true underlying sequence (this is fundamentally a consensus / denoising problem, similar to averaging noisy repeated measurements).

A stat framing you'll like: coverage as a sampling problem

If you randomly shred a genome of length G into reads of length L, and you generate enough reads to total N·L letters of sequence, your average coverage (also called depth) is:

c = (N × L) / G

Because shredding positions are effectively random, the Lander–Waterman model treats the number of reads covering any given position as approximately Poisson-distributed with mean c. The probability that a specific position gets zero reads (a gap) is:

P(gap at a position) = e−c

At c=5, that's e⁻⁵ ≈ 0.7% chance of a gap at any given base — sounds small, but summed across billions of positions that's millions of gapped bases. At c=30, it drops to e⁻³⁰ ≈ 0.0000000009% — vanishingly small. This is exactly why assembly projects target 20–30×, and sometimes 60×+, average coverage rather than something close to 1×.

2 · Sequencing: How You Actually Get the "Reads"

Before any assembly math happens, a physical machine has to turn DNA molecules into digital sequences of letters (A, C, G, T). Different technologies make fundamentally different tradeoffs between read length, accuracy, and cost — and this tradeoff is the single biggest factor determining which assembly algorithm you'll use later.

TechnologyTypical read lengthRaw accuracyKey trait
Illumina (short-read)~150–300 lettersVery high (~99.9%)Cheap, extremely accurate, but too short to resolve most repeats or phase haplotypes on its own
PacBio HiFi (long-read)~15,000–20,000 lettersVery high (~99.9%, i.e. "HiFi" = high fidelity)The current default choice for new high-quality genome projects — long and accurate
Oxford Nanopore (ONT) (long-read)Tens of thousands to over a million letters ("ultra-long")Historically lower, now much improved with newer chemistryBest raw read lengths of any platform — can span even very long repeats — cheaper equipment

How PacBio HiFi gets both long AND accurate (the clever bit)

This seems like it should be impossible — longer reads are usually noisier, not less. The trick: PacBio sequences the same physical DNA molecule multiple times in a circle (it's called circular consensus sequencing), then computationally averages those repeated passes together, the same way averaging repeated noisy measurements of the same quantity shrinks your uncertainty:

SE = σ / √n

More passes (n) over the same molecule → lower final error, same logic as your standard-error formula.

Why not just use one technology for everything?

In practice, the highest-quality modern genome projects (like the Vertebrate Genomes Project / Earth BioGenome Project standards) typically combine multiple data types, because each catches what the others miss:

Long reads (PacBio HiFi or ONT)

Do the heavy lifting of the initial assembly — long enough to resolve most repeats and get a highly contiguous draft.

Hi-C data

Doesn't sequence new DNA per se — instead captures which regions of the genome are physically close together in the 3D-folded nucleus, and is used afterward to order and orient pieces into full chromosomes (Section 7).

As of the most recent Earth BioGenome Project assembly guidance, a common modern recipe is roughly 20× or more PacBio HiFi coverage per haplotype, sometimes supplemented with ONT ultra-long reads and always paired with Hi-C for chromosome-scale scaffolding.

3 · K-mers: The Mathematical Building Block

If there's one concept that unlocks almost everything else in modern genome assembly and QC, it's the k-mer. It's a simple, almost embarrassingly basic idea — which is exactly why it works so well computationally.

Definition

A k-mer is just every substring of length k that appears in a sequence, read with a sliding window of step size 1. If k=4 and your sequence is ACGTAC, the k-mers are: ACGT, CGTA, GTAC — three overlapping 4-letter windows from a 6-letter string. In general, a string of length L has exactly L − k + 1 k-mers.

Try it yourself

Click "Extract k-mers" to see the sliding window in action.

Why k-mers are so useful (three reasons)

  1. They turn a sequence-comparison problem into a set/counting problem. Instead of asking "does this long string look like that long string," you can ask "do these two sets of k-mers overlap a lot" — sets and counts are things computers (and statisticians) handle extremely efficiently at massive scale.
  2. They power de Bruijn graph assembly — an entirely different, elegant graph formulation of the assembly problem (Section 4).
  3. They enable reference-free quality checking via Merqury (Section 11).

The counting intuition behind error-detection

If you sequence a genome at coverage c and count how many times each k-mer appears, most true, real k-mers will show up roughly c times. But k-mers that only exist because of a random sequencing error in a single read will typically show up just once. Plotting "k-mer, how many times seen" produces a histogram with a big peak around c (real sequence) and a small pile near count = 1 (errors).

Idealized k-mer spectrum: two peaks that separate sequencing errors from real genomic content.

4 · Two Graph Formulations of "Put the Pieces Back Together"

This is the algorithmic heart of assembly, and it maps directly onto graph theory you already know: nodes, edges, and finding a path through the graph that reconstructs the original sequence.

Approach A: The Overlap Graph (used for long reads)

Build a graph where each node is an entire read. Draw a directed edge between two reads if their ends overlap by more than some threshold. The genome is reconstructed by finding a path through this graph that visits enough nodes to explain all the reads — essentially a Hamiltonian-path-like problem, solved approximately via graph simplification heuristics.

Approach B: The De Bruijn Graph (used for short reads)

Nodes are k-mers, and you draw an edge between two k-mers if they overlap by k−1 letters. Reconstructing the genome becomes an Eulerian-path-style problem, dramatically cheaper to solve at scale than the Hamiltonian-path-style problem of overlap graphs.

Walking edge-to-edge through this tiny graph and reading the last letter of each edge spells out the original sequence.

Where de Bruijn graphs struggle

A repeated k-mer creates a node the graph walk passes through multiple times — the ambiguity problem from Section 1. This is why de Bruijn-graph short-read assemblies are typically far more fragmented than modern long-read assemblies.

7 · Hi-C: Using 3D Physics to Order 1D Sequence

Inside a cell's nucleus, a chromosome is folded up in 3D, like a tangled ball of string. Hi-C chemically freezes DNA in its folded state, cuts and re-glues nearby pieces together, and sequences the resulting glued junctions.

The statistical principle behind Hi-C

Two DNA locations close together along the same chromosome are, on average, also close together in 3D space, and get glued together far more often than two random locations on different chromosomes. Counting how frequently every pair of DNA locations gets glued together produces a giant, noisy distance/contact matrix — pieces from the same chromosome cluster together strongly, exactly like a Multi-Dimensional Scaling problem.

Darker/brighter clusters near the diagonal reveal which contigs are physically near each other.

8 · Polishing: Cleaning Up Errors After Assembly

Even after contigs and scaffolds exist, the underlying letter-by-letter sequence usually still contains small errors inherited from noisy reads. Polishing is the correction pass.

Take all your original sequencing reads and map them back onto your draft assembly. At each position, if the assembly says "A" but the overwhelming majority of high-quality reads mapped there say "G," that's strong evidence of an assembly error, and it gets corrected.

A subtlety: over-polishing can go wrong

A "correction" can introduce new errors — for example, incorrectly "fixing" a real but rare genetic variant into a more common one because the polishing algorithm effectively voted based on population-level patterns. This is why polishing results are always re-checked with the same quality metrics (Sections 9–11).

9 · Grading the Assembly, Part 1: N50 and Contiguity

N50, defined precisely

Sort all your contigs (or scaffolds) from longest to shortest. Walk down that sorted list, adding up lengths as you go, until your running total reaches 50% of the total assembly length. The length of the specific contig/scaffold you were adding when you crossed that halfway mark is the N50.

Try it yourself: N50 calculator

Click to compute.

Why N50 (and not the average) is the standard

N50 answers: "if I pick a random base pair anywhere in the genome, how long is the contig it's likely sitting in?" — weighted by where sequence actually lives, robust to a pile of tiny fragments in a way a plain average isn't.

The critical limitation

N50 says nothing about correctness or completeness. An assembler that aggressively (and wrongly) merges unrelated sequences can post a high N50 while producing a broken assembly — see Sections 10–11 for the other two lenses.

10 · Grading the Assembly, Part 2: BUSCO and Completeness

N50 tells you about piece sizes; it says nothing about whether biologically important content is actually there. BUSCO (Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs) checks exactly that.

Across a broad group of related species, a curated set of genes exist almost everywhere as exactly one copy each. BUSCO checks, for each expected gene: is it there, and does it look intact and single-copy?

BUSCO categoryWhat it meansWhat it might indicate if elevated

11 · Grading the Assembly, Part 3: QV and Base-Level Correctness

The third question: for any given letter in the assembly, how likely is it to actually be correct? That's QV (Quality Value), most commonly via Merqury.

The formula

QV = −10 × log10(P)

where P is the estimated probability that any given base is wrong. QV 20 ≈ 1-in-100 error. QV 30 ≈ 1-in-1,000. QV 40 ≈ 1-in-10,000 — each +10 in QV is a 10× reduction in error probability, exactly like decibels.

Merqury reuses the k-mer machinery from Section 3: build the set of k-mers from raw, unassembled reads (trusted, since many independent reads confirm real k-mers), then flag k-mers in the finished assembly that never showed up there — evidence of an assembly error.

Rough QV levelError ratePractical meaning
QV 20~1%Not acceptable for a modern high-quality reference
QV 30~0.1%A common older "good enough" threshold
QV 40+~0.01% or betterCurrent Earth BioGenome Project-style standard
QV 60+~0.0001%Near-perfect; best modern T2T assemblies

The trap: high N50 + low QV

It can mean the assembler aggressively merged sequences that don't belong together — high contiguity, quietly broken correctness.

18 · Your Genome vs. "The" Human Genome

This is the confusion you flagged, and it's a genuinely subtle point — even many biology students get it fuzzy. Let's be precise.

🧬 Your genome

The actual, specific sequence of DNA inside your cells. Unique to you (except identical twins). About 3.1 billion letters, in two copies of each chromosome (one from each parent — more in Section 21).

📖 "The" human reference genome

A single, shared, standardized sequence that the scientific community agreed to use as a common coordinate system / "ruler" — so that everyone doing human genetics can describe positions and differences using the same addresses. It is not any specific living person's actual genome, and it is not an "average" human either.

The map analogy

Think of the reference genome like a well-known city map (say, of a major world city). Nobody claims the map is a perfect literal photograph of the city at this exact moment — buildings get renovated, streets get renamed, and each resident's actual house looks slightly different up close. But the map is enormously useful because it gives everyone a shared coordinate system: "meet me at the corner of 5th and Main" only works because we all agree on what "5th and Main" refers to.

The key insight

When your doctor or a research lab "sequences your genome," in almost all modern practice they are not building your personal genome completely from scratch the way Section 1–8 describe. Instead they generate your reads, then align (map) them against the existing reference genome, and report the list of differences (variants) — because describing "here's everywhere you differ from the shared map" is vastly cheaper and more useful than re-deriving the entire 3-billion-letter sequence independently every single time.

So when is a genome actually assembled "from scratch" (de novo)?

De novo assembly (everything in Sections 1–12) is done: (1) once, historically, to build the reference in the first place; (2) periodically, to build an improved/updated reference; (3) for specific research or clinical purposes where reference bias is a concern (e.g., cancer genomes, or a structural rearrangement the reference can't represent); and (4) always, for any species that doesn't have a reference yet (Section 22).

19 · Wait — Is the Reference One Person, or a Blend of Many?

Here's a genuinely surprising, under-told part of the story, and it directly matters for how you should interpret the word "reference."

The real history of the human reference genome (GRCh38 and its predecessors)

The Human Genome Project recruited volunteer donors in the late 1990s, and — contrary to what many donors were told at the time — the resulting reference is not an even blend of many people. Due to how the lab processing worked (based on cloned DNA fragment libraries), the final assembled reference genome (versions still used today, like GRCh38) is a mosaic: different physical stretches of the reference sequence come from different donors, stitched together — and the stitching is wildly uneven. One single anonymous male donor, referred to by the code name RP11, contributes roughly 70% of the entire current human reference genome by sequence length. The rest comes from a patchwork of other donors' clone libraries.

Why this matters, beyond trivia

  • It's not a "typical" or "average" human in any statistical sense — it's an accident of laboratory logistics, dominated by one person's DNA.
  • It has known gaps and errors inherited from how cloned fragments were stitched together, some only fully resolved decades later.
  • It creates "reference bias": because the reference over-represents one ancestry background, people whose DNA differs more from RP11's ancestry can, in some analyses, show up with systematically more "differences" from the reference — not because their DNA is unusual, but because the ruler itself isn't neutral.
  • There's an important, ongoing ethical story here too: donors were told the DNA would be pooled anonymously and no one person would dominate — that assurance didn't hold, and RP11 himself was reportedly never told his DNA became the backbone of the world's most-used genetic reference.

What replaced/supplemented this approach

ResourceWhat it isWhy it exists
GRCh38 (and patches, "hg38")The classic mosaic reference described above, still widely used and continually patched for correctionsHistorical continuity — enormous amounts of existing research, tools, and clinical pipelines are built around it
T2T-CHM13A single, essentially complete, gapless (telomere-to-telomere) genome, but built from one very unusual cell line (a "complete hydatidiform mole," which — due to a quirk of its biological origin — is effectively haploid, containing only one set of chromosomes instead of two, making it much easier to assemble without the diploid tangle from Section 1)To finally close the gaps and fix the structural errors that had persisted in the mosaic reference for two decades

The pangenome, in one picture

Instead of a single line (one sequence), imagine a graph where the "backbone" represents shared sequence common to virtually everyone, and where it branches into alternate parallel paths at positions/regions where real human populations differ substantially — each path representing a real, observed version from actual sampled individuals rather than one dominant donor's version being treated as the default "normal."

20 · How Many Samples Does It Take to Build a Reference?

The honest answer: it depends entirely on what kind of reference you're trying to build — and the number has changed dramatically as the field's ambitions have grown. Here's the full spectrum.

Minimum to build any reference: 1

Technically, you can build a usable, publishable reference genome from a single individual's DNA — this is exactly what most first-ever reference genomes for a new species look like (Section 22), and it's also effectively what the original human reference mosaic amounted to in practice, dominated by one donor.

For a single, complete, gapless reference: 1 (but a special one)

T2T-CHM13 is built from just one cell line — chosen specifically because its unusual haploid biology sidesteps the diploid-assembly tangle from Section 1, making a fully gapless assembly achievable from a single sample.

For a diversity-representing pangenome: dozens+

The current Human Pangenome Reference draft used 47 individuals (94 haplotypes), deliberately chosen to be genetically/ancestrally diverse — enough to capture common structural variation across human populations, though not remotely enough to capture every rare variant that exists globally.

For population-level variant catalogs: thousands

Separate from building the reference sequence itself, projects that catalog how common each genetic variant is across humanity (used for things like disease-risk statistics) sequence thousands to hundreds of thousands of people — a fundamentally different, additional layer of data built on top of a reference, not a replacement for one.

Why more samples matters: a coverage-of-diversity argument, not a coverage-of-bases argument

Note this is a completely different "coverage" concept from Section 1's sequencing coverage. There, more reads reduced the chance of missing a base by chance (P = e⁻ᶜ). Here, more individuals reduces the chance that some real, meaningful variant in the population goes completely unobserved. If a genetic variant exists at frequency f in a population, the probability that none of your n sampled individuals happen to carry it is approximately:

P(variant missed) ≈ (1 − f)2n

(the exponent is 2n because each diploid individual carries two chromosome copies). For a reasonably common variant (f = 5%) and n = 47 individuals (94 haplotypes), P(missed) ≈ 0.95⁹⁴ ≈ 0.8% — you'd almost certainly catch it. But for a rare variant (f = 0.1%), P(missed) ≈ 0.999⁹⁴ ≈ 91% — you'd very likely miss it entirely. This is exactly why a 47-person pangenome is a huge, meaningful step up in representing diversity compared to one donor, while still being nowhere near enough to catalog rare variation — that's what the much larger, thousands-to-hundreds-of-thousands-person variant-catalog projects are separately for.

Direct answer to your question

So: "how many samples to build a reference" doesn't have one number — one sample gives you a usable, working reference (and is exactly the realistic starting point for a new species, Section 22); dozens gives you a reference that reasonably represents common diversity within a species; thousands+ is a different, additional kind of resource (a variant catalog) layered on top, aimed at rare variation. Nearly every reference genome that has ever existed for any species — including essentially every non-human species reference — is built from just one, or a small handful, of individuals.

21 · "One From Each Parent" — Which One Actually Gets Mapped?

This is a wonderfully specific question and it deserves a careful, step-by-step answer, because the honest answer is "it depends which kind of analysis you're doing," and conflating the options is a common source of confusion even among people well into a bioinformatics career.

Step 1: What "one copy from each parent" actually means physically

You have 23 pairs of chromosomes. For each pair, one physical chromosome copy came from your mother's egg cell, and the other came from your father's sperm cell. At any given gene/position, you might have inherited the identical DNA letter from both parents (homozygous) or two different letters (heterozygous). Across your whole genome, the overwhelming majority of positions are homozygous or nearly so (all humans are >99.9% identical to each other) — it's the small minority of heterozygous positions that make you genetically distinguishable from your parents and siblings.

Step 2: What happens when your DNA gets sequenced (the standard, most common case)

Standard sequencing (whether short-read Illumina or long-read) extracts DNA from your cells, which contains both chromosome copies mixed together in the same tube — the machine has no built-in way of knowing which physical read came from your mother's copy versus your father's copy. Both get sequenced and both get mapped, together, onto the reference genome (which itself, recall from Section 19, is just one arbitrary single sequence, not "the maternal" or "the paternal" version of anything).

So, directly answering your question: which one do we map?

In routine sequencing, both get mapped — reads from your maternal copy and reads from your paternal copy both get aligned to the same single reference sequence, and they simply pile up together at every position. At a homozygous position, all the reads (from both copies) agree, and you see one clear consensus letter. At a heterozygous position, the reads at that spot will show roughly 50% one letter and 50% the other — and that specific pattern (a clean, roughly 50/50 split at one position, with normal agreement everywhere else) is precisely the statistical signal used to detect that you're heterozygous there, and to call it as a variant.

Both parental copies get mapped to the same reference position; the 50/50 read split at a position is the signal of heterozygosity.

Step 3: But sometimes you DO want to know which copy is which — that's "phasing"

Knowing that you're heterozygous at two different positions doesn't automatically tell you whether both "unusual" letters came from the same parent (sitting together on one physical chromosome) or from different parents (one on each copy) — and for some purposes (certain disease genetics, or reconstructing full haplotypes) that distinction matters a lot. Recovering it is called phasing, and there are a few ways to do it:

Trio-based phasing

Sequence both parents too. If a child's heterozygous position matches something one parent is homozygous for, you can directly infer which parent contributed which letter.

Long-read phasing

If a single long read is long enough to span two or more heterozygous positions at once, it directly proves those specific letters travel together on the same physical chromosome copy — no parental samples needed.

Population-based statistical phasing

Using patterns of which variants tend to travel together across large reference panels of many other people's already-phased genomes, to statistically infer the most likely phasing for a new individual.

Connecting back to Section 1's "haplotype-aware" assemblers

This is exactly why Section 1 and Section 5 made a big deal of "haplotype-aware" assembly (like hifiasm's phased output): when you're building a brand-new reference genome from scratch (rather than mapping to an existing one), you have the opportunity to keep the maternal and paternal copies fully separate from the very beginning, producing two clean haplotype sequences instead of one blended consensus — which is a strictly richer, more informative result when it's achievable.

So what does "the reference genome" actually represent, in this light?

Now the full picture snaps together: the classic reference (GRCh38) is just one haploid, mosaic sequence — not "the maternal genome" or "the paternal genome" of anyone, and not even one consistent person's real single copy throughout. It's a single arbitrary coordinate backbone. When you map your own sequencing reads to it, you're not asking "does this match my mom's copy or dad's copy" — you're asking "does this match the reference's one arbitrary sequence, or not," at every position, for reads coming from both of your own copies simultaneously.

22 · You Want to Build a Reference Genome for a New Species. Where Do You Start?

Let's make this fully concrete and practical — a real checklist, in the order decisions actually get made.

1Decide what "the species" even means for your purposes, and pick one individual (usually). Since Section 20 established that one sample is a perfectly normal, standard starting point, your first real decision is just: which individual? Common considerations: avoid an obviously sick or unusual individual, consider sex (some projects specifically want a female to avoid the added complexity of divergent sex chromosomes, or specifically want a male to capture a species' Y or W chromosome), and — importantly — try to get a wild-type or representative individual rather than a captive one with unusual/admixed ancestry if that's a concern (this was flagged for the tortoise cell line in your earlier document).

2Get high-quality, high-molecular-weight DNA. This is a real, practical bottleneck: long-read sequencing (Section 2) needs long, intact DNA molecules going in — if your extraction method shears the DNA into short fragments before it even reaches the sequencer, you've thrown away the main advantage of modern long-read technology before you started. Fresh or properly flash-frozen tissue is far better than degraded or long-preserved samples.

3Estimate the genome size first (often with a quick, cheap short-read k-mer analysis, using the k-mer-spectrum idea from Section 3 — the total area under that "true k-mer" peak, divided by the coverage, estimates genome size) so you know how much long-read sequencing you'll actually need to hit your target coverage (Section 1's coverage formula).

4Sequence with PacBio HiFi and/or ONT at your target coverage, and ideally also generate Hi-C data from the same individual (Section 7) for proper chromosome-scale scaffolding — this is much stronger than borrowing a relative's structure (Section 14's synteny caveat).

5Assemble using a modern assembler matched to your data type (Section 5), then scaffold with Hi-C (Section 7), then polish (Section 8).

6Quality-check along all three axes — N50, BUSCO, QV (Sections 9–12) — using a BUSCO gene set from the closest available well-studied lineage (e.g., "vertebrata" or "sauropsida" for a reptile) since a species-specific curated gene set essentially never exists yet for a brand-new species.

7Annotate (Section 13) — this is often the hardest step for a genuinely new species, since there's no existing RNA/gene evidence for that species yet; typically this means generating fresh RNA-seq data from real tissue of the organism specifically to help find genes, supplemented by comparing against related, better-studied species.

8Publish and deposit the assembly (with full metadata on sequencing tech, coverage, assembler, and QC metrics) in a public genome database, so it becomes available as the working reference for that species going forward — exactly the role GRCh38 plays for humans, now filled for this new species by whatever you just built.

The honest, important caveat

A "reference genome" for a new species, built this way, is really a first draft — one individual's genome, pressed into service as everyone's shared coordinate system, exactly analogous to how RP11 became the backbone of the human reference almost by accident (Section 19). It will very likely get revised, improved, or eventually supplemented by a pangenome as more individuals of that species get sequenced and the community's needs grow — this is a normal, expected life cycle for a reference genome, not a sign the first one was done wrong.

23 · Does the Process Differ for Animals, Bacteria, and Viruses?

Substantially, yes — mostly driven by three factors that cascade through everything else: genome size, ploidy (how many chromosome copies), and genome structure/complexity.

Why bacteria are often "easier"

Two of Section 1's three enemies are largely absent or muted: bacteria are haploid (no diploid-blending problem), and their genomes, while dense with genes, generally have far fewer long, complex repeats than animal genomes — so a single circular contig, gapless and complete, is a routine, almost mundane outcome from a single good long-read sequencing run, not a multi-year flagship project like a new animal reference often is.

Why viral "assembly" can look completely different

For a virus a few thousand to a hundred-thousand letters long, a single modern long read can sometimes span the entire genome in one piece — at that point, "assembly" in the graph-theoretic sense from Section 4 may barely be needed at all; it can become more of a consensus-and-error-correction problem across many reads that each already cover the whole thing, rather than a stitching-together problem. This is part of why viral genome sequencing (relevant to your earlier ZIKV/LGTV project) is comparatively fast and routine compared to a new animal genome project.

Where it gets more complex again: viral populations and quasispecies

Because viruses replicate fast and error-prone, a single infected sample doesn't actually contain one uniform viral genome — it contains a genetically diverse swarm of closely related variants (sometimes called a "quasispecies"). So while assembling a representative consensus viral genome is easy, characterizing the full diversity of viral variants present in one sample is its own specialized, much harder sub-problem — conceptually a bit like the pangenome-diversity problem from Section 19–20, but playing out within a single infected host rather than across a species.

One unifying takeaway

Every organism type ultimately runs through the same conceptual pipeline from this guide — sequence, assemble, scaffold if needed, polish, QC, annotate — but the difficulty dial for each step is set almost entirely by genome size, ploidy, and repeat content. Animals sit at the hard end of that dial; bacteria are moderate; small viruses sit at the easy end, sometimes skipping steps (like scaffolding) entirely because there's nothing left to scaffold.

13 · Annotation: Finding the Genes in the Finished Sequence

A finished, high-quality assembly is still just a very long string of A/C/G/T letters — it doesn't come with labels. Annotation is the separate process of figuring out where the genes actually are, and what they do.

Two complementary annotation strategies

Evidence-based (RNA-seq-guided)

Sequence actual RNA molecules being made by real cells of the organism, then align those RNA sequences back onto the genome. Wherever RNA reliably aligns is strong, direct evidence a gene is being actively transcribed right there.

Prediction-based (ab initio / homology)

Use statistical models trained on known gene structures to predict likely genes directly from raw sequence, and/or compare against genes already known in related species. More uncertain — a probabilistic guess rather than a direct observation.

Why annotation quality lags genome quality in non-model organisms

Annotation is generally harder and less reliable than the underlying sequence assembly, especially for a species that isn't a heavily studied "model organism." Two specific failure modes matter:

  • Gene family collapse: genes existing in multiple similar copies (paralogs) can get blurred together by automated pipelines.
  • Liftover errors: transferring annotations from an older assembly or related species carries along any of their errors.

14 · Cross-Species Scaffolding & Liftover, Explained Properly

This directly unpacks the technique used to build CheloAbing_2.0 from your original tortoise document — different from, and weaker evidence than, the native Hi-C approach from Section 7.

The core assumption: synteny

Synteny is the observation that large blocks of gene order are often conserved across related species — genes near each other on a chromosome in one species often still sit near each other, in the same relative order, in a related species.

Where it can mislead you

Real evolutionary events — chromosome fusions, splits, large inversions — do happen, and exactly at those locations, the "borrow the relative's order" assumption breaks down. A rescaffolded genome can look confidently chromosome-scale while containing subtle structural errors precisely where the two species' real structures diverge.

15 · The Full Pipeline, Start to Finish

1Collect a high-molecular-weight DNA sample from the organism, from one representative individual (Section 22).

2Sequence using PacBio HiFi and/or ONT for long reads (Section 2), plus Hi-C data (Section 7).

3Assemble contigs using a modern, ideally haplotype-aware assembler (Sections 4–5).

4Scaffold into chromosome-scale sequences using Hi-C (Section 7), or synteny to a relative if Hi-C isn't available (Section 14).

5Polish to correct remaining base-level errors (Section 8).

6Quality-check along N50, BUSCO, and QV (Sections 9–12).

7Annotate the sequence to identify genes (Section 13).

8Release the genome as the working reference (Section 18) — understanding it's a first draft representing one individual (Sections 19–20), that future sequencing of other individuals will map against it rather than re-assemble from scratch, and that it may eventually be joined by a pangenome as more samples accumulate.

16 · Glossary

Read

A single sequenced fragment of DNA.

Coverage / depth

Average number of reads overlapping a given position.

K-mer

Every substring of length k from a sequence, via sliding window.

Contig

A continuous, gap-free stretch of assembled sequence.

Scaffold

Contigs joined in inferred order/orientation, with gaps.

Haplotype / phasing

One of the two parental chromosome copies; phasing keeps them separate rather than blended.

N50 / L50 / NG50

Contiguity metrics from sorted contig/scaffold lengths.

BUSCO

Completeness check against a curated set of universal single-copy genes.

QV (Quality Value)

Phred-scaled estimate of base-level correctness.

Merqury

Reference-free tool estimating QV via k-mer comparison to raw reads.

Hi-C

Technique capturing 3D physical proximity of DNA, used for chromosome-scale scaffolding.

Synteny

Conservation of gene order across related species.

Polishing

Post-assembly error correction using mapped reads.

Annotation

Identifying gene locations/functions in a finished genome.

Liftover

Transferring annotations from one assembly/species to another.

Telomere-to-telomere (T2T)

A gapless assembly spanning a full chromosome end to end.

Reference genome

A single, standardized, shared sequence used as a common coordinate system for a species — not necessarily any one real individual's complete genome (Section 18).

Mosaic reference

A reference built by stitching together sequence from multiple different donors, as GRCh38 was (Section 19).

Pangenome

A reference structure built from many individuals' genomes, representing population diversity rather than one linear sequence (Section 19).

Variant / heterozygous site

A position where an individual's two chromosome copies differ, or where an individual differs from the reference (Section 21).

Alignment / mapping

Finding where a sequencing read best matches within a reference genome, rather than assembling from scratch.

Ploidy

The number of complete chromosome sets an organism carries — most animals are diploid (2), most bacteria/viruses are haploid (1).

Plasmid

A small, usually circular piece of DNA in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome.

Quasispecies

The genetically diverse swarm of closely related viral variants present within a single infected host.

17 · Quick Self-Test

Questions covering both the original assembly mechanics and the new reference-genome concepts.

1. Why do longer sequencing reads generally produce better (less fragmented) assemblies?

Correct: length specifically helps resolve repeats.

2. What does N50 primarily measure?

Correct: N50 is purely about contiguity. BUSCO = completeness, QV = correctness.

3. Is the human reference genome (GRCh38) an average or "typical" human genome?

Correct: it's a mosaic, dominated by the RP11 donor, and neither an average nor one clean individual.

4. When your own DNA is sequenced and mapped to the reference, whose chromosome copy (maternal/paternal) gets mapped?

Correct: both copies get sequenced and mapped together; a ~50/50 split of reads at a position reveals heterozygosity. Telling which copy is which requires a separate step called phasing.

5. How many individual samples are needed to build a usable reference genome for a brand-new species?

Correct: one individual is the standard starting point. Diversity-representing pangenomes (dozens of individuals) and population variant catalogs (thousands+) are separate, later additions.

6. Why are bacterial genomes generally much easier to assemble into a single, complete, gapless contig than animal genomes?

Correct: haploidy removes Enemy 2 from Section 1, and lower repeat content reduces Enemy 1 — the two main sources of assembly difficulty.
